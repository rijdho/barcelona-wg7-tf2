# Making Research Processes Visible: A Taxonomy for the Open Research Information Era

## White Paper on the Necessity and Design of a Comprehensive Stakeholder-Benefit Taxonomy

**Barcelona Declaration Working Group 7 - Task Force 2**

**Version:** 1.0
**Date:** January 2026
**Authors:** WG7-TF2 Community

---

## Executive Summary

For decades, research evaluation has been dominated by simple output metrics—papers published, projects funded, citations accumulated. These metrics are easy to count but fail to capture the complex, collaborative, and process-oriented nature of modern research. **The paradox is stark: while research has become more interconnected, interdisciplinary, and socially engaged, our ability to see and value these dimensions has diminished.**

This white paper argues for a fundamental shift in how we conceptualize research information and its benefits. We present a comprehensive taxonomy that makes visible what has long been hidden: the diverse stakeholders engaged in research, the multifaceted roles they play, and the broad spectrum of value created throughout the research lifecycle—not just at its publication endpoint.

**Key insights:**
- Traditional metrics have systematically obscured the research **process**, privileging only measurable **outputs**
- This invisibility has created perverse incentives, undermining research quality, integrity, and societal relevance
- The Barcelona Declaration's call for open research information (ORI) offers an unprecedented opportunity to make research processes transparent and valued
- A structured taxonomy is essential to operationalize this vision, enabling stakeholders to understand, communicate, and realize the benefits of ORI

Our proposed taxonomy identifies **eight benefit dimensions**, **eleven stakeholder categories**, and **eight functional roles**, providing a flexible framework that accommodates the diversity of the global research ecosystem while enabling systematic evidence collection and advocacy.

## 1. The Crisis of Invisibility in Research

### 1.1 What Traditional Metrics Conceal

Research evaluation in the 20th century was shaped by scarcity: limited journal space, localized research communities, and high information distribution costs. In this context, counting publications and citations was a practical, if imperfect, proxy for research quality and impact.

But **the digital revolution has fundamentally changed research**, while our evaluation systems have remained stuck in the past. Modern research is characterized by:

- **Distributed collaboration** across institutions, countries, and disciplines
- **Complex workflows** involving data collection, processing, analysis, validation, and dissemination
- **Diverse outputs** including data, software, protocols, and educational materials
- **Multiple impact pathways** beyond academic citation, including policy, practice, and public engagement
- **Continuous evolution** with preprints, versioning, post-publication review, and living documents

Traditional metrics—publications, citations, h-indices, impact factors—**make all of this invisible**. They reduce the rich, multidimensional activity of research to a single moment: publication. Everything that comes before (research design, data collection, ethical review, collaboration, skill development) and everything that comes after (reuse, translation, societal impact) is rendered invisible.

### 1.2 The Hidden Costs of Invisibility

This invisibility is not merely an academic concern—it has **profound consequences** for research quality, integrity, and relevance:

#### **1.2.1 Undermining Research Quality**
When only publications count, researchers have incentives to:
- Slice work into "least publishable units" rather than comprehensive studies
- Prioritize novelty over replication and validation
- Hide negative results that could save others years of wasted effort
- Rush to publication rather than ensuring data quality and reproducibility

**The invisible process**: Data quality assurance, methodological rigor, transparent reporting—all the practices that ensure research reliability—are invisible in publication counts.

#### **1.2.2 Distorting Collaboration and Credit**
Traditional authorship conventions:
- Obscure the diverse contributions of team members
- Disadvantage researchers in support roles (data stewards, research software engineers, methodologists)
- Create conflicts over author order and corresponding authorship
- Make interdisciplinary work difficult to evaluate fairly

**The invisible process**: The collaborative work of building shared understanding, resolving conflicts, integrating perspectives, and synthesizing knowledge across disciplines.

#### **1.2.3 Devaluing Societal Engagement**
When only academic citations matter:
- Research questions are shaped by publishing trends rather than societal needs
- Public communication and stakeholder engagement are seen as "distractions"
- Translation of research into practice and policy is someone else's problem
- Communities affected by research have no voice in its design or evaluation

**The invisible process**: The ongoing dialogue between researchers and communities, the adaptation of research to local contexts, and the long-term work of building trust and capacity.

#### **1.2.4 Hiding Ethical and Integrity Issues**
Publication metrics cannot reveal:
- Questionable research practices (p-hacking, HARKing, selective reporting)
- Conflicts of interest and funding bias
- Ethics violations or research misconduct
- Lack of diversity and inclusion in research teams

**The invisible process**: Ethical deliberation, transparency in funding and conflicts, inclusive team building, and accountability mechanisms.

#### **1.2.5 Obscuring Learning and Development**
Academic careers are evaluated on outputs, not growth:
- Early-career researchers' learning trajectories are invisible
- Failed experiments and "negative" results are hidden, preventing organizational learning
- Capacity building in under-resourced regions is undervalued
- Methodological innovation is risky unless it leads to quick publications

**The invisible process**: Skill development, mentorship, methodological experimentation, and institutional capacity strengthening.

### 1.3 The Meeting Problem: Why Do We Need So Many Meetings?

A revealing symptom of the invisibility crisis is the **proliferation of meetings** in research organizations. When processes are invisible, **understanding requires constant verbal explanation**:

- Evaluators need meetings to understand what researchers actually did
- Funders need meetings to understand project progress beyond milestone reports
- Administrators need meetings to understand resource needs and bottlenecks
- Collaborators need meetings to understand each other's methods and data
- Policy makers need meetings to understand research relevance and implications

These meetings are necessary because **our information systems don't make the research process visible**. We have rich metadata about publications, but almost none about:
- Research design decisions and their rationale
- Data collection protocols and quality assurance
- Analytical choices and sensitivity analyses
- Collaborative workflows and contribution tracking
- Skill development and capacity building
- Stakeholder engagement and ethical deliberation
- Translation and implementation efforts

In a world with open, structured research information, many of these conversations could be **asynchronous, documented, and reusable**. The same information wouldn't need to be explained repeatedly; it would be discoverable, traceable, and connected.

## 2. The Barcelona Declaration's Vision: Making Processes Visible

### 2.1 What is Open Research Information?

The [Barcelona Declaration on Open Research Information](https://barcelona-declaration.org/), launched on April 16, 2024, represents a paradigm shift in how we conceptualize and manage research information. It calls for:

> **"Openness as the default for research information"**—structured, machine-readable metadata about all stages and outputs of the research lifecycle, made openly available for reuse.

This is fundamentally different from traditional bibliographic databases. Open Research Information (ORI) encompasses:

- **Research inputs:** Funding, facilities, equipment, collaborations
- **Research processes:** Protocols, workflows, ethical approvals, data management
- **Research outputs:** Publications, data, software, educational materials, patents
- **Research impacts:** Citations, reuse, policy influence, public engagement, societal outcomes
- **Research actors:** Researchers, institutions, funders, infrastructure providers, communities

Critically, ORI is:
- **Structured:** Following standards and schemas that enable computation and aggregation
- **Interoperable:** Connected across systems through persistent identifiers (PIDs)
- **Open:** Accessible without barriers for transparency and reuse
- **Comprehensive:** Covering the full research lifecycle, not just publication endpoints

### 2.2 The Four Commitments of the Barcelona Declaration

Signatories pledge to:

1. **Make openness the default** for research information they use and produce
2. **Work with services and systems** that support open research information
3. **Support the sustainability** of infrastructures for open research information
4. **Support collective action** to accelerate the transition to openness

These commitments recognize that ORI is not just a technical infrastructure challenge—it's an **ecosystem transformation** requiring coordinated action by diverse stakeholders with different needs, capacities, and incentives.

### 2.3 Why a Taxonomy? The Challenge of Collective Action

The Barcelona Declaration creates a **collective action challenge**: How do we mobilize a diverse, global community with different priorities to invest in a shared vision?

Traditional advocacy approaches focus on demonstrating benefits to a single stakeholder group—typically researchers or funders. But ORI is a **multi-sided platform**: its value increases as more stakeholders participate, but each stakeholder type benefits differently.

**A taxonomy solves this coordination problem by:**

1. **Making benefits explicit:** Clearly articulating what each stakeholder gains from ORI
2. **Revealing interdependencies:** Showing how different roles and benefits connect
3. **Enabling targeted communication:** Allowing advocates to speak to each stakeholder's concerns
4. **Structuring evidence collection:** Providing a framework for systematic case study gathering
5. **Informing policy design:** Ensuring policies address diverse stakeholder needs
6. **Facilitating comparison:** Enabling stakeholders to learn from peers in similar roles

Without a taxonomy, advocacy remains ad hoc and anecdotal. With a taxonomy, we can build a **cumulative evidence base** that grows stronger with each case study and makes the collective benefits of ORI increasingly clear.

### 2.4 The Global Context: Converging Reform Movements

The Barcelona Declaration does not exist in isolation. It is part of a **global convergence** of initiatives aimed at transforming research culture, assessment, and information systems. This taxonomy is designed to support and complement these parallel efforts:

#### **Plan S and the Open Access Movement**
[Plan S](https://www.coalition-s.org/), launched in 2018 by cOAlition S—a consortium of research funders from across Europe and beyond—mandates immediate open access to publications from publicly funded research. With effect from 2021, all scholarly publications from cOAlition S-funded research must be published under open licenses, preferably Creative Commons Attribution (CC BY).

**The 2026-2030 Strategy** takes a more flexible, multi-pathway approach, recognizing that "no single model can meet all needs" while maintaining commitment to a more inclusive, equitable, and sustainable publishing system.

**Connection to this taxonomy:** Plan S addresses **what** should be open (publications); the Barcelona Declaration addresses **what metadata** about research should be open. Our taxonomy helps stakeholders understand the benefits of open research information that enables compliance, monitoring, and enhanced discoverability of Plan S publications (Benefits B1, B4, B7).

#### **Coalition for Advancing Research Assessment (CoARA)**
[CoARA](https://www.coara.org/), launched in December 2022, brings together over 700 organizations committed to reforming research assessment. The [Agreement on Reforming Research Assessment](https://www.coara.org/agreement/the-agreement-full-text/) establishes 10 core commitments emphasizing:
- Recognition of diverse research outputs and practices
- Qualitative evaluation supported by responsible use of quantitative indicators
- Transparency in assessment criteria and processes
- Equity and inclusiveness in evaluation

**Connection to this taxonomy:** CoARA establishes **principles** for assessment reform; this taxonomy provides the **stakeholder and benefit framework** for implementation. Our eight benefit dimensions (B1-B8) operationalize CoARA's vision by identifying what should be valued beyond publication counts. Particularly, B5 (Evaluation for Learning) directly supports CoARA's emphasis on formative assessment.

#### **Open Science Monitoring Initiative (OSMI)**
[OSMI](https://open-science-monitoring.org/), emerging from a December 2023 UNESCO conference in Paris and coordinated by Université de Lorraine, SPARC Europe, and UNESCO, establishes [Principles of Open Science Monitoring](https://open-science-monitoring.org/the-principles-of-open-science-monitoring-are-now-online/) grounded in three pillars:
1. **Relevance and significance:** Monitoring serves meaningful purposes aligned with open science goals
2. **Transparency and reproducibility:** Methods and data are openly documented
3. **Self-assessment and responsible use:** Monitoring supports learning, not punitive ranking

**Connection to this taxonomy:** OSMI defines **how to monitor** open science; this taxonomy defines **what benefits to track** and **for which stakeholders**. Our benefit dimensions provide concrete categories that can be monitored using OSMI-compliant approaches. The alignment is particularly strong in B5 (Evaluation for Learning) and B8 (Integrity & Accountability).

#### **PathOS - Open Science Impact Pathways**
[PathOS](https://pathos-project.eu/) was a three-year EU Horizon Europe project (Grant Agreement 101058728, 2022-2025) dedicated to identifying and quantifying the multifaceted impacts of open science across scientific, societal, and economic domains. The project leveraged big data and AI to map causal pathways, using datasets like OpenAIRE Graph (180M publications, 4M projects) and PATSTAT (200M+ patents).

The project has concluded, with results now available through the [Open Science Impact Indicator Handbook](https://handbook.pathos-project.eu/) and the PathOS Open Science Resources Hub, providing practical guidance for measuring open science impacts.

**Connection to this taxonomy:** PathOS provides **quantitative impact measurement methods**; this taxonomy provides the **stakeholder-benefit framework** for interpreting those impacts. PathOS identified impact pathways; our taxonomy identifies who benefits at each stage. The two are highly complementary: PathOS indicators can be organized by our benefit dimensions, and our stakeholder map identifies whose impacts were measured by PathOS.

#### **The Synergy: Why These Initiatives Need Each Other**

These initiatives address different but interdependent aspects of research system transformation:

| **Initiative** | **Primary Focus** | **Taxonomy Contribution** |
|----------------|-------------------|---------------------------|
| **Plan S** | Open access to publications | Identifies stakeholders needing metadata for compliance, discovery (B1, B4) |
| **CoARA** | Research assessment principles | Operationalizes principles through multi-dimensional benefits (B1-B8) |
| **OSMI** | Monitoring methodologies | Provides benefit categories to monitor and stakeholder perspectives to include |
| **PathOS** | Impact measurement tools | Supplies stakeholder framework for organizing and interpreting impact data |
| **Barcelona Declaration** | Open research information | Provides the metadata infrastructure enabling all other initiatives |

**This taxonomy serves as a connecting layer**, helping translate between these initiatives and enabling institutions to see how investments in open research information support multiple reform agendas simultaneously.

## 3. Design Principles for the Taxonomy

### 3.1 Overarching Goals

The taxonomy must be:

- **Comprehensive:** Cover the full diversity of stakeholders and benefits in the global research ecosystem
- **Structured:** Provide clear hierarchies and relationships that aid navigation and understanding
- **Flexible:** Accommodate regional, disciplinary, and institutional variations
- **Evidence-based:** Ground categories in empirical observation and literature
- **Actionable:** Support practical use in communication, policy, and system design
- **Evolving:** Allow for community-driven refinement and expansion

### 3.2 Multi-dimensional Architecture

Traditional classifications are typically single-dimensional: stakeholders are defined by their institutional type (university, funder, publisher). But this **flattens the complexity of how actors engage with ORI**.

Our taxonomy adopts a **multi-dimensional architecture:**

```
BENEFIT DIMENSIONS (What value is created?)
       ↕
STAKEHOLDER CATEGORIES (Who participates?)
       ↕
FUNCTIONAL ROLES (How do they interact with ORI?)
       ↕
SPECIFIC STAKEHOLDERS (Concrete actors)
```

This architecture recognizes that:
- A single stakeholder (e.g., a university) can play **multiple roles** (producer, evaluator, support provider)
- The same benefit (e.g., research quality) can be valued for **different reasons** by different stakeholders
- Benefits are not delivered in isolation but emerge through **interactions between roles**

### 3.3 Benefit-Centered Design

A critical design choice: **We organize the taxonomy around benefits, not stakeholders.**

Why? Because:

1. **Benefits drive adoption:** Stakeholders engage with ORI because they expect value
2. **Benefits enable comparison:** Different stakeholders can seek the same benefit through different means
3. **Benefits reveal gaps:** If a benefit is articulated but no stakeholder prioritizes it, we've identified a coordination failure
4. **Benefits guide measurement:** Each benefit suggests its own evaluation criteria and evidence types

Traditional stakeholder maps often list actors and their interests without clear structure. By centering benefits, we create a **value-oriented framework** that naturally connects stakeholder needs to ORI capabilities.

### 3.4 Role-Based Flexibility

Rather than assuming each stakeholder category has a fixed relationship to ORI, we introduce **functional roles** that cut across categories:

- **Producer:** Creates research outputs and metadata
- **Evaluator:** Assesses quality and impact
- **Enabler:** Provides infrastructure and services
- **Policy Maker:** Sets governance frameworks
- **Support & Stewardship:** Maintains quality and provides training
- **Advocate / Civil Actor:** Promotes accessibility and relevance
- **Coordinator / Facilitator:** Connects actors and aligns practices
- **Data Analyst:** Generates insights from research information

This role-based approach:
- **Recognizes multi-functionality:** Most stakeholders play several roles
- **Enables cross-learning:** Stakeholders in the same role but different categories can share practices
- **Clarifies responsibilities:** Roles suggest specific actions and contributions
- **Facilitates coalition-building:** Stakeholders in similar roles have natural affinities

### 3.5 Hierarchical But Not Rigid

The taxonomy provides **hierarchical structure** (benefits → categories → roles → specific stakeholders) to aid comprehension, but it's not a strict tree. Relationships are **networked:**

- Roles map to multiple benefits
- Stakeholders perform multiple roles
- Benefits reinforce each other (e.g., improved metadata quality enables better evaluation)
- The taxonomy can be navigated from any entry point (start with a stakeholder, a role, or a benefit)

This networked structure mirrors the **actual complexity** of the research ecosystem while still providing enough structure to guide action.

## 4. The Eight Benefit Dimensions: Addressing Historical Blind Spots

Each benefit dimension in our taxonomy addresses a specific **blind spot** of traditional research metrics.

### **B1: Research Output Quality & Traceability**
**Traditional blind spot:** Metrics count publications but don't assess reproducibility, data quality, or provenance.
**ORI enables:** Complete tracking from research question through data collection, analysis, and publication, enabling replication and building on prior work.
**Why it matters:** Science advances through cumulative knowledge building, which requires transparency about methods, data, and analytical choices.

### **B2: Governance, Ethics & Compliance**
**Traditional blind spot:** Ethical conduct and regulatory compliance are invisible in publication counts.
**ORI enables:** Transparent tracking of ethics approvals, data protection, conflict of interest declarations, and institutional policies.
**Why it matters:** Public trust in research depends on demonstrable integrity and accountability, especially in sensitive domains like health and privacy.

### **B3: Capacity Building & Inclusive Training**
**Traditional blind spot:** Learning processes and skill development are absent from traditional metrics.
**ORI enables:** Documentation of training, mentorship, and capacity building, especially supporting underrepresented groups and regions.
**Why it matters:** Sustainable research ecosystems require continuous skill development and must address historic inequities in access to training and resources.

### **B4: Interoperability & Metadata Quality**
**Traditional blind spot:** The infrastructure enabling research connectivity is invisible in traditional metrics.
**ORI enables:** Adoption of standards, PIDs, and metadata schemas that allow systems to communicate and data to flow across platforms.
**Why it matters:** Modern research is inherently collaborative and cross-institutional, requiring seamless information exchange.

### **B5: Evaluation for Learning & Responsibility**
**Traditional blind spot:** Traditional metrics drive competitive ranking rather than organizational learning and improvement.
**ORI enables:** Multi-dimensional assessment that values process quality, equity, and societal alignment, not just output quantity.
**Why it matters:** Evaluation should support institutional learning and strategic development, not just justify existing hierarchies.

### **B6: Visibility, Equity & Societal Relevance**
**Traditional blind spot:** Societal impact and public engagement are systematically undervalued in metrics that privilege academic citations.
**ORI enables:** Tracking of research accessibility, community engagement, policy influence, and alignment with societal priorities like SDGs.
**Why it matters:** Research is a public good, funded largely by public resources, and must demonstrate relevance to societal challenges.

### **B7: Infrastructure & Technical Enablement**
**Traditional blind spot:** The technical foundations enabling research (repositories, PIDs, platforms) are completely invisible in traditional output metrics.
**ORI enables:** Recognition and support for the infrastructure that makes open research possible.
**Why it matters:** Infrastructure requires sustained investment; its value must be visible to justify and sustain funding.

### **B8: Integrity & Accountability**
**Traditional blind spot:** Traditional metrics cannot reveal ethical issues, conflicts of interest, or questionable research practices.
**ORI enables:** Audit trails, transparent reporting, and mechanisms for detecting and addressing misconduct.
**Why it matters:** Research integrity cannot be assumed; it must be demonstrated through transparent documentation and accountability systems.

### 4.1 Benefit Interdependencies

These benefits are **mutually reinforcing**:
- Improved metadata quality (B4) enhances traceability (B1) and enables better evaluation (B5)
- Capacity building (B3) improves governance (B2) and infrastructure (B7)
- Visibility and equity (B6) depend on interoperability (B4) and integrity (B8)

This reinforcement creates a **virtuous cycle**: early investments in ORI generate multiple benefits, which justify further investment, which unlocks additional benefits.

## 5. Stakeholder Categories and Roles: Recognizing Diversity

### 5.1 Why Eleven Categories?

Our taxonomy identifies **eleven stakeholder categories**, significantly more than typical typologies (which often distinguish only researchers, funders, and publishers). This granularity is necessary because:

1. **Different institutional contexts** create different incentives, constraints, and opportunities
2. **Policy interventions** must be tailored to specific stakeholder circumstances
3. **Evidence collection** requires understanding stakeholder-specific outcomes
4. **Coalition building** benefits from recognizing natural affinity groups

The eleven categories span:
- **Research performers:** Universities, research centers, individual researchers
- **Supporters:** Funders, libraries, research managers
- **Enablers:** Infrastructure providers, publishers, technology companies
- **Governors:** Policy makers, evaluators, accreditation bodies
- **Connectors:** Intergovernmental organizations, networks, alliances
- **Society:** Civil society organizations, patient groups, citizen scientists

### 5.2 The Power of Roles

By introducing **eight functional roles**, we:
- **Avoid stereotyping:** Recognize that universities, for instance, are not just "producers" but also evaluators, policy makers, and infrastructure providers
- **Enable cross-learning:** A university library and a national infrastructure provider both play "enabler" roles and can learn from each other
- **Clarify responsibilities:** When designing ORI systems, each role suggests specific requirements and contributions
- **Identify gaps:** If a critical role (e.g., "support & stewardship") is under-resourced, the taxonomy makes this visible

### 5.3 From Categories to Concrete Actors

The taxonomy moves from abstract categories to **concrete stakeholders** (e.g., "universities" → "Universitat de Barcelona"). This specificity is essential for:
- **Case study collection:** Real examples involve specific institutions
- **Targeted communication:** Advocacy materials can be tailored to named stakeholders
- **Peer learning:** Institutions can identify peers in similar contexts
- **Accountability:** Signatories to the Barcelona Declaration are specific institutions, not abstract categories

## 6. Operationalizing the Taxonomy: From Theory to Practice

### 6.1 Evidence Collection and Case Studies

Working Group 7's mandate includes collecting case studies demonstrating ORI benefits. The taxonomy provides a **structured framework** for this:

**For each case study:**
1. **Identify stakeholders:** Which actors were involved? What categories and roles?
2. **Map benefits:** Which benefit dimensions were realized? With what evidence?
3. **Document processes:** How was ORI created, shared, and used?
4. **Capture outcomes:** What changed? For whom? How do we know?
5. **Extract lessons:** What enabled success? What were barriers?

This structured approach ensures case studies are **comparable and cumulative**, building an evidence base that grows more persuasive with each addition.

### 6.2 Stakeholder Engagement and Communication

The taxonomy enables **targeted advocacy:**

**For universities:**
"ORI enhances your research quality and traceability (B1), strengthens your governance and compliance (B2), and supports your capacity-building mission (B3). As producers, evaluators, and stewardship providers, you play multiple critical roles."

**For funders:**
"ORI improves your evaluation processes (B5), ensures integrity and accountability (B8), and demonstrates societal relevance (B6). Your policy-making and evaluation roles make you a key driver of change."

**For infrastructure providers:**
"ORI depends on your enabler role. Benefits include improved interoperability (B4), technical advancement (B7), and research quality (B1). Your sustainability is central to the entire ecosystem."

**For civil society:**
"ORI increases research accessibility and societal relevance (B6), enhances integrity and accountability (B8), and improves quality (B1). Your advocate role ensures research serves public needs."

### 6.3 Policy Design and Implementation

Effective ORI policies must address **multi-stakeholder needs**. The taxonomy helps by:

1. **Identifying affected stakeholders:** Who will this policy impact?
2. **Assessing role implications:** How does the policy affect different roles (producers vs. enablers vs. evaluators)?
3. **Mapping benefit delivery:** Which benefits does the policy unlock? For whom?
4. **Designing incentives:** How can policy align incentives across stakeholder groups?
5. **Planning capacity building:** Which stakeholders need training and support?

Example: A funder mandating ORI deposition must consider:
- **Producers (researchers):** Need clear guidelines, training, and infrastructure access
- **Enablers (repositories):** Need resources to handle increased deposits and ensure quality
- **Evaluators (assessment agencies):** Need capacity to use ORI in evaluation processes
- **Support & Stewardship (libraries):** Need training to assist researchers with compliance

The taxonomy ensures **no stakeholder is forgotten** in policy design.

### 6.4 Infrastructure Development

When building ORI systems, the taxonomy guides **user-centered design:**

1. **Identify user categories and roles:** Who will use this system? How?
2. **Map required functionalities:** What capabilities does each role need?
3. **Design for multi-role users:** Recognize that single users (e.g., researchers) may perform multiple roles
4. **Enable benefit measurement:** Build in analytics to demonstrate benefit delivery
5. **Plan for interoperability:** Connect to systems serving other stakeholder roles

Example: A CRIS (Current Research Information System) serves:
- **Producers:** Need intuitive deposit interfaces and version control
- **Support & Stewardship:** Need curation tools and quality dashboards
- **Evaluators:** Need reporting and analytics capabilities
- **Policy Makers:** Need aggregate statistics and compliance tracking
- **Enablers (external systems):** Need robust APIs for data exchange

## 7. Addressing Challenges and Limitations

### 7.1 Complexity vs. Usability

**Challenge:** A comprehensive taxonomy can be overwhelming. How do users navigate 11 stakeholder categories, 8 roles, and 8 benefits?

**Mitigation strategies:**
1. **Multiple entry points:** Users can start with their stakeholder category, role, or benefit of interest
2. **Layered presentation:** Offer overview diagrams before detailed descriptions
3. **Use cases and examples:** Illustrate concepts with concrete scenarios
4. **Interactive tools:** Develop digital interfaces for exploring relationships
5. **Simplified summaries:** Provide executive summaries for different audiences

### 7.2 Global Diversity and Local Contexts

**Challenge:** Research ecosystems vary dramatically across regions, disciplines, and institutional types. Can a single taxonomy accommodate this diversity?

**Mitigation strategies:**
1. **Flexible granularity:** Allow for subcategories and local variations
2. **Regional case studies:** Ensure evidence base reflects global diversity
3. **Community-driven evolution:** Enable local communities to propose additions and refinements
4. **Disciplinary adaptations:** Acknowledge that stakeholder roles and benefits may vary by field
5. **Multi-language development:** Ensure taxonomy is accessible in major research languages

### 7.3 Maintaining Currency

**Challenge:** The research ecosystem evolves rapidly. How do we keep the taxonomy current?

**Mitigation strategies:**
1. **Version control:** Clear versioning to track changes over time
2. **Community governance:** Establish process for proposing and reviewing changes
3. **Regular review cycles:** Annual reviews to assess relevance and completeness
4. **Evidence-based updates:** Ground changes in empirical observation and case studies
5. **Deprecation process:** Clear procedures for retiring obsolete categories or benefits

### 7.4 Avoiding Over-simplification

**Challenge:** Reality is messier than taxonomies suggest. How do we avoid implying false clarity?

**Mitigation strategies:**
1. **Acknowledge complexity:** Explicitly note that stakeholders play multiple roles and seek multiple benefits
2. **Document exceptions:** Include case studies that don't fit neatly into categories
3. **Networked, not hierarchical:** Emphasize connections and relationships, not rigid boundaries
4. **Provisional categories:** Mark emerging stakeholder types or benefits as provisional
5. **Qualitative depth:** Complement taxonomy with rich case study narratives

## 8. Expected Impacts of the Taxonomy

### 8.1 For the Barcelona Declaration Community

**Near-term (2026):**
- Structured framework for Working Group 7's case study collection
- Common language for cross-national and cross-institutional dialogue
- Evidence base for demonstrating ORI benefits to diverse stakeholders

**Medium-term (2027):**
- Comprehensive case study library organized by taxonomy dimensions
- Stakeholder engagement materials tailored to each category and role
- Integration with emerging research assessment reform initiatives (DORA, CoARA)

**Long-term (2028+):**
- Global recognition of the taxonomy as standard for ORI benefit mapping
- Policy frameworks explicitly referencing taxonomy categories
- Infrastructure designs incorporating taxonomy-informed user needs

### 8.2 For Research Institutions

**Strategic planning:**
- Clearer articulation of how ORI investments deliver institutional benefits
- Ability to benchmark against peers in similar stakeholder categories
- Evidence to justify ORI infrastructure and capacity building investments

**Communication and advocacy:**
- Compelling narratives for engaging leadership, faculty, and funders
- Demonstration of institutional contributions to global research ecosystem
- Evidence for research assessment and reputation enhancement

### 8.3 For Funders and Policy Makers

**Policy development:**
- Comprehensive stakeholder analysis for ORI mandates and incentives
- Understanding of multi-stakeholder dependencies and coordination needs
- Framework for designing equitable and effective ORI policies

**Evaluation and impact:**
- Multi-dimensional assessment of ORI initiatives beyond publication metrics
- Ability to track benefits across different stakeholder groups
- Evidence for justifying sustained investment in ORI infrastructure

### 8.4 For Infrastructure Providers

**Service design:**
- User-centered design informed by stakeholder roles and needs
- Interoperability requirements derived from multi-stakeholder workflows
- Value propositions tailored to different customer segments

**Sustainability:**
- Articulation of infrastructure value across multiple benefit dimensions
- Evidence for funding proposals and business development
- Community engagement strategies aligned with stakeholder priorities

### 8.5 For Researchers

**Career development:**
- Recognition of diverse research contributions (data, software, training) beyond publications
- Visibility of collaboration, mentorship, and capacity-building activities
- More equitable evaluation incorporating process quality and integrity

**Research practice:**
- Incentives aligned with research quality, not just publication quantity
- Infrastructure and support for open, reproducible research
- Connection to societal stakeholders and impact pathways

## 10. Conclusion: From Invisibility to Visibility

For too long, research has been evaluated by what's easiest to count, not what matters most. **Publications and citations are visible; processes, collaboration, integrity, and societal engagement are not.** This invisibility has distorted incentives, undermined quality, and obscured the true value of research to society.

The Barcelona Declaration's vision of open research information offers a historic opportunity to **make the invisible visible**. But realizing this vision requires more than technical infrastructure—it requires a shared understanding of:
- **Who** benefits from ORI
- **How** they benefit
- **Why** their participation matters
- **What** they need to succeed

This is what the taxonomy provides: a structured, comprehensive, and actionable framework for understanding and advancing the open research information ecosystem.

The transformation won't happen overnight. Building ORI infrastructure, changing institutional practices, and shifting evaluation cultures are long-term endeavors. But every case study collected, every stakeholder engaged, and every policy aligned with taxonomic principles brings us closer to a research system that:

- **Values process as well as output**
- **Recognizes diverse contributions and roles**
- **Connects research to societal needs**
- **Builds on cumulative, transparent knowledge**
- **Supports learning and continuous improvement**

The research ecosystem is complex, but it is not incomprehensible. With the right frameworks—like this taxonomy—we can navigate complexity without oversimplifying, recognize diversity without fragmenting, and coordinate action without centralized control.

**The alternative to a comprehensive taxonomy is not freedom from categorization—it's reliance on inadequate, implicit categories that privilege some stakeholders and benefits while rendering others invisible.**

By making our frameworks explicit, structured, and open to refinement, we create the conditions for a more equitable, effective, and trustworthy research system. One where research processes are as visible and valued as research products. One where diverse contributions are recognized. One where societal relevance is not an afterthought but a fundamental dimension of research excellence.

This is the promise of open research information. This taxonomy is a step toward realizing it.

## Appendix: Frequently Asked Questions

### **Q1: Isn't this taxonomy too complex for practical use?**
**A:** Yes and no. The full taxonomy is comprehensive, but users rarely need all of it at once. You typically start from one entry point—your stakeholder category, your role, or a benefit you're seeking—and navigate to related elements. Think of it like a map: detailed but navigable. We're also developing simplified visual guides and interactive tools.

### **Q2: How does this relate to existing research assessment reform initiatives like DORA and CoARA?**
**A:** This taxonomy complements and operationalizes those initiatives. DORA and CoARA establish principles (don't use journal metrics inappropriately, assess research on its own merits, value diverse contributions). Our taxonomy provides a structured framework for implementing those principles by identifying who benefits, how, and what information is needed.

### **Q3: My institution/region/discipline is different. Does this taxonomy still apply?**
**A:** The taxonomy is designed to be flexible and adaptable. The core structure (benefits, categories, roles) is intended to be universal, but specific stakeholders and implementation details will vary. We encourage regional and disciplinary communities to adapt and extend the taxonomy for their contexts while maintaining core compatibility.

### **Q4: How do I get started using the taxonomy?**
**A:** Start with your own context:
1. Identify your stakeholder category and role(s)
2. Review the benefits most relevant to your situation
3. Look for case studies from similar stakeholders
4. Use the framework to articulate your own ORI value proposition
5. Contribute your experiences back to the community

### **Q5: Who maintains and updates the taxonomy?**
**A:** The taxonomy is maintained by Barcelona Declaration Working Group 7, Task Force 2, with community input. Proposed changes are reviewed regularly and incorporated through a transparent process. Contact wg7-taskforce2@barcelona-declaration.org to contribute.

### **Q6: How does this taxonomy relate to technical metadata standards like Dublin Core, DataCite, or CERIF?**
**A:** This taxonomy operates at a different level. Technical metadata standards define how to structure and encode information. Our taxonomy defines what information is valuable and for whom. The two are complementary: technical standards enable implementation; our taxonomy guides what should be implemented and why.

### **Q7: Is this taxonomy only for signatories of the Barcelona Declaration?**
**A:** No, the taxonomy is openly available for anyone working with research information. While it was developed in the Barcelona Declaration context, its principles and structure are applicable to any organization seeking to understand or enhance the value of research information.

### **Q8: What if my organization doesn't fit neatly into one stakeholder category?**
**A:** Most organizations don't! That's why we have functional roles. A university, for example, is in the "Research-Performing Institutions" category but performs Producer, Evaluator, Support & Stewardship, Enabler, and sometimes Policy Maker roles. The multi-dimensional structure accommodates this complexity.

### **Q9: How can I contribute to the evidence base?**
**A:** Submit case studies through the Working Group 7 channels. Document:
- Your stakeholder type and role
- The ORI practices you implemented
- The benefits you observed (mapped to taxonomy dimensions)
- Evidence of those benefits
- Lessons learned and recommendations

### **Q10: What's next for the taxonomy?**
**A:** Short-term priorities include:
- Collecting and analyzing case studies using the framework
- Developing stakeholder-specific guidance documents
- Creating interactive visualization and exploration tools
- Translating into multiple languages
- Integrating with policy development processes

Join Working Group 7 to participate in these developments!
