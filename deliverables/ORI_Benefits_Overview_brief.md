# The Value of Open Research Information: Ten Transformative Benefits

**Barcelona Declaration Working Group 7 - Task Force 2** **Version:** 0.5 (Brief) **Date:** February 2026, revised August and November 2026

**Open research information (ORI)** is structured, machine-readable metadata about research actors, activities, inputs, outputs and impacts, made openly available through open infrastructures that use standard protocols and identifiers.

The Barcelona Declaration commits signatories to: (1) **making openness the default**, (2) **working with open systems**, (3) **supporting infrastructure sustainability**, and (4) **enabling collective action**.

This brief describes ten benefits that follow from those commitments. Each one states what traditional metrics miss, what open research information adds, and who benefits.

## **The ten benefits, in three axes**

### **AXIS I: QUALITY & TRUST**

#### **B1: Research Output Quality & Traceability**

Traditional metrics count publications and say little about how the research was done. Open metadata on publications, research data, software, samples and instruments, linked to each other, show what a research output rests on and what was made from it. Researchers and others can then find the outputs that matter to them, assess them, and check and repeat the work.  
**Who benefits:** Researchers can find and verify each other's work and build on it; societal stakeholders can find and assess the research that concerns them; institutions can show quality by more than citations; funders can follow the outputs of what they fund.

#### **B2: Governance, Ethics & Compliance**

Publication counts do not show whether research follows the rules that apply to it. With open research information, an institution can show that its research meets ethical and data protection requirements, and an institution, a funder or a government can check whether its policies, for instance on open access, are met, with figures that others can check too. Not all research information can be open: information that cannot be shared ethically, such as information with privacy implications, stays closed. The principle is "as open as possible, as closed as necessary".  
**Who benefits:** Research institutions can show that they work responsibly; funders and governments can check whether their policies are met; anyone who uses the figures can verify them.

#### **B8: Integrity & Accountability**

Traditional metrics do not show what information a decision about research rests on. When research information is open, those who are assessed can see and audit the information used about them, information on funding and grants shows who funded a study, and it is visible where the information comes from and how it was processed. Open information also helps to detect misconduct and conflicts of interest.  
**Who benefits:** Researchers and institutions can audit the information by which they are assessed; evaluators can find integrity problems early; civil society can see who funded research and how it is held to account.

### **AXIS II: COLLABORATION & INNOVATION**

#### **B3: Capacity Building & Inclusive Training**

Traditional metrics do not record what people learn. Open research information shows training in open research practices and in FAIR data, programmes that teach how to work with metadata, support for researchers from under-represented groups, and the capacity an institution builds.  
**Who benefits:** Early-career researchers are recognised for the skills they acquire; under-represented communities receive support directed at them; institutions build lasting capacity for open science.

#### **B4: Interoperability & Metadata Quality**

Traditional metrics do not show whether the information about research is complete, correct and connected. Open research information promotes harmonised metadata schemas, persistent identifier (PID) adoption, standardised vocabularies, system integration and APIs, and cross-platform data exchange. Because the information is open, those who use it can check it against its source and report what is wrong.  
**Who benefits:** Infrastructure providers ensure system interoperability; researchers access data across institutional boundaries; libraries and data stewards maintain high-quality metadata; services that reuse the information can check it against its source.

#### **B7: Infrastructure & Technical Enablement**

Traditional output metrics do not show the technical services that research depends on. Open research information supports those services and the organisations that run them: infrastructure that can be sustained, platforms that stay available, and new tools for research.  
**Who benefits:** Technology providers can build platforms that last; organisations that run research infrastructure are recognised for work that others depend on; institutions can justify what they invest in infrastructure.

#### **B9: Strategic Steering & Institutional Autonomy**

Traditional metrics say nothing about what research costs or about who supplies the information used to decide. Open research information gives institutions two means to steer. (a) It makes the scholarly communication system itself observable: the costs and terms of publishing and the conduct and performance of venues. Institutions can then reallocate resources towards community-led and Diamond infrastructure, and researchers can choose where to publish on visible terms. (b) It reduces dependency on proprietary data sources and supplier lock-in: institutions build their own analyses, indicators and tools, fitted to their own strategy, instead of taking a supplier's.  
**Who benefits:** Institutions decide where to invest on their own evidence rather than a supplier's; libraries and research managers can justify a budget shift and work with indicators fitted to their institution; community-led and Diamond infrastructures become fundable on visible grounds; researchers choose venues on transparent terms; funders and policy makers see the real economics of scholarly communication.

#### **B10: Reuse & Collective Action**

Traditional metrics count what each organisation produces, not what others build with its information. Open research information can be built on by others: one organisation's open data becomes the input of another's service, practices spread between peer institutions, and shared infrastructure can be run by alliances rather than bought separately.  
**Who benefits:** Service builders start from existing open data instead of collecting it again; institutions join shared initiatives that others opened; alliances and networks share the cost of running infrastructure.

### **AXIS III: IMPACT & RELEVANCE**

#### **B5: Evaluation for Learning & Responsibility**

Traditional metrics are used to rank researchers and institutions against each other, not to learn from the results. Open research information makes other kinds of assessment possible: formative assessment, evaluation of impact in several dimensions, evaluation that attends to equity, and assessment from which an organisation learns. These are the practices of responsible research assessment.  
**Who benefits:** Evaluators can assess the quality of research and not only count outputs; institutions learn what works and why; researchers receive feedback that helps them develop, and not only a verdict.

#### **B6: Visibility, Equity & Societal Relevance**

Traditional metrics give little weight to the effect of research on society and to public engagement. Open research information makes research easier to find and use outside academia, supports citizen science and public engagement, makes research from more regions and in more languages visible, and shows how research relates to the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs).  
**Who benefits:** Civil society organisations can reach the research that concerns them; citizen scientists can contribute; policymakers can connect research with what society needs; communities in more parts of the world can take part in research.

## **Summary**

| Benefit | What traditional metrics miss | What open research information adds |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **B1: Research Output Quality & Traceability** | How the research was done | Outputs that can be found, traced and assessed through open metadata |
| **B2: Governance, Ethics & Compliance** | Whether research follows the rules that apply to it | Compliance that can be shown, and checked with figures open to others |
| **B8: Integrity & Accountability** | What information a decision about research rests on | Information that those assessed can audit, with its funding and its origin visible |
| **B3: Capacity Building & Inclusive Training** | What people learn | Training and capacity on record |
| **B4: Interoperability & Metadata Quality** | Fragmented systems, information that cannot be checked | Data exchange, information checked against its source |
| **B7: Infrastructure & Technical Enablement** | The technical services research depends on | Services that can be sustained, and recognition for those who run them |
| **B9: Strategic Steering & Institutional Autonomy** | What publishing costs, and who supplies the information used to decide | Decisions on an institution's own evidence, with its own indicators and tools |
| **B10: Reuse & Collective Action** | What others build with an organisation's information | Services built on open data, infrastructure run by alliances |
| **B5: Evaluation for Learning & Responsibility** | What can be learned from the results | Assessment that institutions and researchers can learn from |
| **B6: Visibility, Equity & Societal Relevance** | The effect of research on society | Research that people outside academia can find and use |

## **Changes in 0.4 (25 November 2026)**

- **B1, B2 and B8 are rewritten around research information.** The Barcelona Declaration
  defines research information as "information (sometimes referred to as metadata) relating
  to the conduct and communication of research". The texts of these three benefits
  described research itself: reproducible results, ethics approvals, misconduct. The
  Declaration mentions none of the three, and in the six case responses B1 and B8 were
  reported by none and B2 by one.
- **B1** now says what open metadata on publications, data, software, samples and
  instruments let people do with a research output: find it, see what it rests on and
  assess it. The Declaration calls research information "indispensable for researchers and
  societal stakeholders to find and assess relevant research outputs".
- **B2** now says that an institution, a funder or a government can check whether its
  policies are met, with figures that others can check too. It keeps ethics and data
  protection, and adds the limit the Declaration sets: "Research information that cannot be
  ethically shared, including information that has privacy implications, should not be made
  open."
- **B8** now says that those who are assessed can audit the information used about them.
  The Declaration says that open research information "enables information used in
  research evaluations to be accessible and auditable by those being assessed". B8 also
  says that information on funding shows who funded a study and that the origin of the
  information is visible. Detecting misconduct is kept from v0.1.
- **The line between B4 and B8.** B4 is about whether the information is correct and who
  can correct it. B8 is about who can see what a decision rests on. One passage moves from
  a question mark to B8: a journal index that shows the source of its metadata.

These are proposals of one member put to the task force. They were written on 25 November
2026 and nobody else has read them. With the new texts, one of the six responses reports
B8 and one B2; none reports B1 or B3.

## **Changes in 0.3 (November 2026)**

- **Added B10: Reuse & Collective Action** (Axis II, Sustainable Innovation). Five of the
  six responses in the WG7 case study collection describe something no benefit named:
  peer institutions following one university into a shared initiative on publishing costs,
  a platform built and paid for by an alliance of universities, an open citation
  infrastructure whose data others build services on, a journal recommender that runs
  on two open sources, and a journal index that checks its records against another
  organisation's open registry. In each, one organisation gains from what another opened, or
  several gain by building together. Four of the five were read on 14 November 2026, and
  the fifth on 23 November, when its story was published.
- **B9 now names its two parts.** (a) is the publishing system made observable. (b) is
  independence from proprietary data sources, which v0.2 mentioned only as something to
  observe. One university library reports building tools and a metric of its own, fitted to
  its strategy, the metric to replace a proprietary one.
- **Narrowed B7** by removing "technical standards implementation" and "service
  interoperability", which overlapped with B4. The line between the two: B4 is the
  information itself (metadata, identifiers, links between records); B7 is the service that
  holds it and the organisation that runs it.
- **B4 now says what its name announces** (25 November 2026). Its text listed schemas,
  identifiers, vocabularies and data exchange, and said nothing of the quality of the
  information. Three responses name quality, traceability or replicability and do not mean
  research outputs: journal records checked against the registry they come from, a national
  platform that cleans the data of its sources, and bibliometric analyses that a library
  can replicate. B4 now opens with the information and adds that open information can be
  checked against its source. B1 stays with research outputs.
- **The other descriptions were reworded** (25 November 2026). Those of B1, B2, B3, B5,
  B6, B7 and B8 were lists of noun phrases after "ORI enables". They are now sentences that
  say who can do what. What each benefit covers was kept as in v0.1, and the summary table
  was rewritten from the new sentences.

Like those of v0.2, these changes are proposals put to the task force. They rest on six
self-selected responses, read once and without a second coder. That shows where the
framework had no place for what a case reports; it does not show how often each benefit
occurs. B1, B3 and B8 were left as they are, although no response reports them as their
text defines them. Six responses are too few to tell a benefit that is not needed from one
that nobody wrote about.

## **Changes in 0.2 (August 2026)**

- **Added B9: Strategic Steering & Institutional Autonomy** (Axis II, Sustainable
  Innovation). The brief did not use the word "strategy" once, and its only economic term
  was "institutions justify infrastructure investments". It described what open information
  makes visible about research, and had no place for information about the system that
  publishes research, nor for an institution acting on it.
- **Narrowed B2** by removing "open science policy implementation". That clause absorbed
  almost any institutional case, because almost every institutional case is a policy being
  implemented. B9 takes what that clause had been holding.

Both came out of placing a real case against the framework: the University of Milan
response in the WG7 case study collection, on nine years of APC monitoring and the funding
decisions it drove. Five things that the case reports fitted no benefit: the transparency
of costs, the move away from proprietary sources, the conduct of a publisher, the choice of
venue, and budget moved to Diamond publishing. B9 was written to hold them.
